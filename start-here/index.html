When a full life needs a new direction

What to protect, what to make room for,
and what to release.

By Dan Strader

The wish underneath the wish

When life shifts, it can be hard to separate what hurts from what you want next. A relationship ends. Work that once absorbed you feels thin. A business falters. Or life looks good from the outside, and you keep wondering why you want something different.

I keep coming back to a question: what deserves this next part of my life? I can stay busy almost indefinitely. Deciding where that energy belongs takes more attention.

Consider someone who says they want a simpler life. They may need rest and fewer demands. They may also be willing to work hard on something whose purpose they can feel. Understanding the wish matters: an empty calendar alone may leave the deeper question unanswered.

Someone else may want adventure, intimacy, or a different way to inhabit their body after a long relationship ends. The next chapter can be expansive. It can also begin quietly, while they attend to the practical consequences of what has changed.

What needs protecting?

A new direction has to fit the life that exists. Income, a place to live, health, and people who rely on us can make some choices urgent and others premature. The room to experiment will differ from person to person, and from one season to another.

I want my contribution to rest on a foundation I can sustain. Helping someone become more capable matters to me. So does retaining the freedom to step back. A commitment that depends indefinitely on my availability deserves careful thought.

Before adding something new, ask: what needs my attention now so that I have more freedom later? Finishing an obligation or recovering some stability can be meaningful work in its own right.

What do you want more of?

Think of a recent moment when you were glad to be spending your life as you were. What was happening? Perhaps you were absorbed in learning, helping someone get unstuck, making something, or enjoying the company of a person who met you with curiosity.

Be specific about the experience you want. An impressive project can be a poor substitute for companionship. More achievement may do little for a wish to feel physically alive. A small, honest experiment can tell you more than a grand plan built around the wrong need.

For me, a garden and an old car offer learning and pleasure in making. A tool for another creator offers a different satisfaction: something I build becomes useful in someone else's work. I want room for both.

The same question belongs in our use of technology. As AI changes what we can do, I want to ask what that capability is for: what it helps us learn, contribute, or make room for in a life we actually want.

What can change or end?

Past enthusiasm can become a present obligation. A project may have taught us something valuable even if we no longer want it to organize our time. A role may have mattered deeply and still be ready to change.

Letting go has consequences. Some things need finishing, a handover, or a conversation. The useful question is what remains worth the effort from here, with those consequences included.

We can respect what a part of our life has meant and make a different choice about its future.

One choice to learn from

Write down one thing to protect, one thing you want more of, and one commitment to reconsider. Then choose a small action that fits your actual time, energy, and resources.

It might be a thoughtful conversation, an hour on something you care about, or a practical step toward finishing an obligation. Afterwards, ask whether it brought you closer to what you wanted, and what it cost.

That gives you something real to learn from. The next part of a life can take shape through choices like these, alongside all the uncertainty and possibility still ahead.
